The Companies Behind D.C.’s Sports Betting Deal Will Pay $6.5 Million Over Charges They Misled Regulators

Intralot and VSC have agreed to pay to settle a D.C. investigation into their handling of the tortured contract.
by Alex Koma January 14th, 2025January 14th, 2025

Share this story:

  • Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook
  • Share on X (Opens in new window) X
  • Share on Reddit (Opens in new window) Reddit

At last, the companies behind D.C.’s failed sports betting regime are facing some consequences for the long-held allegations at the heart of the tortured deal: That they misled the District to win the lucrative contract. 

Intralot and its top subcontractor on the sports betting deal, Veterans Services Corporation, have agreed to pay the city a combined $6.5 million to settle a case pursued by Attorney General Brian Schwalb over alleged violations of D.C. contracting laws. Although the two companies do not admit wrongdoing, they both promised not to repeat their practices that gave rise to Schwalb’s investigation in their other business with the city. That’s a relevant detail considering that both have won quite a bit of work from D.C. over the years.

Schwalb got the two firms to pay up after arguing that they skirted the city’s minority contracting requirements. In its application for the initial contract, Intralot promised that VSC—theoretically, a small, locally based business—would do most of the work associated with running the city-backed sports betting program. But VSC, run by political insider Emmanuel Bailey, existed mostly on paper and didn’t have the resources to perform the functions it promised, according to the attorney general’s office. Instead, Intralot provided everything to operate the city’s sports betting app via a subsidiary “in exchange for return payments from VSC,” according Schwalb’s office.

“This is a warning to any company that tries to manipulate and exploit District contracting laws, especially laws intended to build the capacity of the local businesses vital to our economy,” Schwalb wrote in a statement. “Intralot and VSC’s sports betting deal was a sham from the start—an elaborate scheme to secure a lucrative, high-profile opportunity on a sole-source basis while circumventing the District’s small business contracting laws.”

These allegations are representative of an all-too-common shell game run by big companies hoping to avoid the city’s small business set-aside rules. The city requires a certain portion of each big contract to flow to smaller local firms, dubbed “Certified Business Enterprises.” But often, large firms partner with local business that are essentially straw companies in order to win city business and then hide that the larger company is actually doing the work. It’s so common, in fact, that councilmembers, activists, and journalists suspected that Intralot and VSC were planning this sort of arrangement as District officials crafted the original sports betting contract back in 2019. 

Nevertheless, lawmakers rushed through a no-bid, sole-source deal, only to watch Intralot and its partners flounder as companies in Maryland and Virginia hoovered up wagers from D.C. bettors. The city has since opened up its sports betting marketplace to private firms as well, essentially admitting that its attempts to run a single, government-backed sportsbook with Intralot were a failure. 

Both companies denied any wrongdoing in their respective agreements. VSC is represented by well-connected lawyer Claude Bailey of Venable, while Intralot hired former U.S. attorney for D.C. Ron Machen, now at Wilmer Hale. An Intralot spokesperson wrote in a lengthy statement that “the conduct in the settlement agreement in no way reflects what [Schwalb’s office] is now characterizing in its release” and it “denies strongly that it engaged in any deception.” Both firms also stressed in statements that they chose to settle only to avoid further costly legal action.

“It is disappointing that the D.C. Attorney General misunderstands the law and attacks a highly experienced, minority-owned small business that has played an integral part in generating more than $3 billion in total revenue and delivering nearly $728 million to the District’s general fund over the last 15 years,” VSC wrote in a statement.

Schwalb’s office has been investigating these two firms since at least last summer and likely earlier, based on court filings first reported by Loose Lips. The Office of the Attorney General’s lawyers wrote in a news release that they found “Intralot and VSC inflated the amount of money Intralot spent subcontracting” with the small firms, even though Intralot was doing most of the work to run the city’s sports betting app, Gambet DC, and betting kiosks installed in local bars. Intralot then paid Bailey “hundreds of thousands of dollars per year for his participation” in the scheme, while VSC would funnel any money Intralot paid the company right back to the Greek gaming firm.

The OAG attorneys also note that D.C. regulators began raising red flags about this arrangement as far back as 2021. LL received hundreds of documents via a public records request in June 2023 supporting this assertion, as the Department of Small and Local Business Development (which monitors minority contracting compliance) began raising concerns about VSC’s performance. At the time, Intralot executives blamed VSC for failing to do the work it promised and even asked to have the subcontractor kicked out of the deal.

When the city refused, Intralot revised its figures and told D.C. officials that VSC had sent something like $4.3 million back to the company, according to OAG. Even after Intralot raised concerns about its own subcontractor, the two continued trying to mislead regulators. OAG estimates that Intralot and VSC submitted more than 100 “fraudulent invoices” to city agencies by 2021. (Intralot argued in its statement that it disagreed with DSLBD’s concerns, but still “worked cooperatively with the agency” to try and address them.)

Intralot has now agreed to pay $5 million in restitution to the District, while VSC will pay $1.5 million. For comparison, Intralot has received close to $18 million in payments from the city tied to this contract over the past three years alone, according to oversight documents submitted to the D.C. Council.

The settlement also requires the two firms to accurately report contracting information going forward and they’ve both sworn off similar practices in any current or future deals with the city. The agreement does not directly mention, however, the two firms’ ongoing work running D.C.’s lottery, which the pair has operated for the past 15 years.

“Most importantly, in deciding to settle this case, the company can now dedicate its full time and attention to continuing the delivery of its exceptional products and services to its US customers, including the District of Columbia,” an Intralot spokesperson wrote.

LL supposes it’s a good thing that Schwalb has secured some recompense for this catastrophic contract. But, ultimately, it is hard to shake the sense that anything will really change.

Most of the officials and lawmakers pushing this contract have either retired to cushy private sector gigs or stayed firmly ensconced in office. LL would be hard-pressed to find anyone in D.C. government who doesn’t believe the CBE system needs reform, but efforts to do so have never gone anywhere. If the worst that happens to companies running this scam are a few fines more than five years after winning a big contract, why upset the status quo?

This story has been updated with comment from Intralot and VSC.
